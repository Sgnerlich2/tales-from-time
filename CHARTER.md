# Tales From Time CHARTER.md

Nov 1, 2026 · @Steven

Approved by Steven, Nov 1, 2026. This text becomes CHARTER.md in the repo, and every model step that pitches, writes, or checks an episode reads it. It is drawn from dossier section 3 and adapted to the handoff; where the two differ, the handoff wins, and the differences are listed at the end.

## Purpose

Tales From Time explains why something in the past happened the way it did. Each episode is about 8 minutes, carries one thesis, and shows its evidence on screen. Every factual sentence traces to a verbatim quote in a saved source.

## Audience

- Curious adults who watch history to understand cause and to follow a good story, not for exam prep or background noise.
- They want the human-scale detail that changed a larger outcome: a missed signal, a bad calculation, a bureaucrat's delay.
- They reject "everything you know is a lie" clickbait and long encyclopedia recitals.
- Our promise: a real puzzle in the first 30 seconds, resolved with evidence the viewer can see, in a runtime that respects their time.

## Launch formats

| Format | What the episode does | Where the tension comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. One decision and its consequences | Places a named person at a decision point and follows what the choice set in motion | The options they had, what they knew, and what they missed |
| 2. An invention, accident, or logistical problem | Explains a concrete technical or logistical failure or success | Physical and practical constraints, shown with maps and diagrams |
| 3. A popular claim tested against evidence | States a well-known claim, labels it as such, then weighs the record | Competing sources shown side by side on screen |

Not at launch: an overlooked person at a pivotal moment, and a familiar event from a less familiar perspective (later candidates, each its own decision). Rejected: everyday life during an extraordinary period, because it lacks a narrative arc and drifts into lists.

## Scope

- Events from 1600 to 1930. Works published before 1931 are public domain in the US, and the era has strong English-language records.
- English-language or well-translated primary records must exist. A translation is cited with its translator.
- Strong fits: maritime, industrial, infrastructure, science, public health, diplomacy, and military logistics stories with digitized inquiries, court records, patents, or parliamentary debates.
- Excluded at launch: atrocities and mass killing, contested religious history, and current geopolitics (any subject still argued over in today's politics). The pipeline stops and asks Steven when a topic touches one of these.
- Minimum to pitch a topic: at least 2 primary and 2 scholarly sources, and visuals for most of the runtime. Minimum, from the dossier: at least 12 usable visual anchors (maps, portraits, documents, period images).
- No repeat of a topic already covered, checked against the channel index.

## Structure

Every script follows five acts, organized around agency, constraint, and consequence rather than chronology. Pace is 140 to 145 words per minute, so an 8-minute episode is about 1,100 to 1,200 words. A script must land within 10 percent of its target length.

| Act | Time (8 min) | Words | Job |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I. The anomaly (hook) | 0:00 to 0:45 | 100 to 115 | Confirm the title's promise. Put a named person in a concrete time, place, and dilemma. Pose the causal question without giving away the answer. |
| II. The constraints (context) | 0:45 to 2:30 | 240 to 260 | Only what the protagonist knew, feared, or lacked at the moment of decision. No biographical throat-clearing. Introduce the main map or diagram. |
| III. The friction (escalation) | 2:30 to 5:15 | 380 to 410 | The sequence of choices, accidents, or competing testimony. Primary quotations appear here. Show where people miscalculated or where records disagree. |
| IV. The cascade (consequence) | 5:15 to 7:00 | 240 to 260 | The immediate outcome, then the second-order effect that makes the moment matter. |
| V. The verdict (payoff) | 7:00 to 8:00 | 130 to 150 | Answer the opening question. Say what the evidence proves and what remains uncertain. Close on an observation, not a call to action. |

A short chapter card marks each act change and feeds YouTube's chapter list.

## Voice

An authoritative, observant investigator: lucid, concrete, dryly witty when human folly earns it, and restrained when describing suffering. Plain modern English, no period pastiche.

| Rule | Do | Never |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quotations | Voice only verbatim text from a saved source, with attribution: According to \[name\]'s testimony at \[inquiry\], ... | Invent dialogue, or paraphrase inside quotation marks |
| Inner life | State thoughts or feelings only from the person's own letter, diary, or sworn testimony: In a letter dated \[date\], \[name\] wrote that ... | Say what someone felt, feared, or secretly intended without such a source |
| Language | Keep prose accessible and period-neutral | Modern jargon, therapy-speak, internet idioms, or anachronistic metaphors |
| Judgment | Explain people within what their era knew and the incentives they faced; let the facts carry the moral weight | Lecture the viewer or mock the past for not knowing what we know |
| Certainty | Match confidence to evidence (see the certainty ladder below); name a historian when a view is one historian's | Present a hypothesis or a partisan chronicle as settled fact |
| Specifics | Named people, dates, places, distances, prices, counts with units | Abstract nouns and unnamed actors where the record names them |
| Hooks | Contrast what was expected with a documented anomaly, and back the hook with claims | Promise a twist, conspiracy, or certainty the record cannot support |
| Myths | Introduce a popular story only as unverified tradition, labeled from its first mention | State a myth as fact in Act I and retract it in Act IV |
| Packaging | Title and thumbnail promise only what the episode shows; thumbnail text does not repeat the title | Clickbait, or a thumbnail image the episode never uses |
| Endings | End on what the evidence shows and why it matters | Like and subscribe as the closing line |

## Suffering

- Describe death, injury, famine, and violence through documented facts: counts, dates, places, and named people where records allow.
- No lurid adjectives, no voyeuristic detail, no music swells for effect, and no graphic images of bodies.
- When a casualty figure is disputed, give the range and say who counted and why their counts differ.
- The people who suffered are people, not plot devices. The episode does not use their deaths as a hook.

## Uncertainty and conflicting evidence

- When claims conflict (they share a conflict group in claims.json), the script must say so, give the range, and explain why the sources differ: who wrote them, how long after the event, and what the author stood to gain.
- Never choose the most dramatic figure. Never hide a disagreement to keep the story clean.
- Act V states what is proven and what is open.
- Primary sources are evidence of what their authors said, not automatically of what happened. Dispatches inflate enemy numbers, official inquiries protect institutions, and memoirs written decades later reshape blame.

Certainty ladder (added by Duet, mapped to the evidence strength field in claims.json):

| Evidence | Allowed wording |
| --- | --- |
| Strong: two independent primary records, or primary plus scholarly agreement | Plain statement of fact |
| Moderate: one primary record, or scholarly consensus without independent primary support | Attributed: According to \[source\], ... |
| Weak or contested | Hedged and ranged: Accounts differ; \[A\] put it at X, \[B\] at Y. |

## Sourcing and citation

- The pipeline finds sources and extracts claims. Steven does not curate sources (D7). Automated verification therefore carries the accuracy burden, and the pilot must prove it before script review is reduced.
- Two citable tiers only. Primary: archival records, testimony, official inquiries, court and parliamentary records, patents, letters, diaries, period newspapers. Scholarly: peer-reviewed work and university-press books.
- Tertiary sources (Wikipedia, Britannica, blogs, AI summaries, popular history sites) may point the way to real sources but are never cited.
- Model memory is never a source. No fact enters a script unless it is in a saved source.
- At least 2 primary and 2 scholarly sources before scripting.
- Every source is saved locally with metadata, a SHA-256 hash, and a bias note: the author's relation to the event, the time between event and record, and the author's incentive.
- Two sources count as independent only if they trace back to different underlying records. Five websites repeating one anecdote are one source.
- Every factual sentence cites at least one claim. Every claim carries a verbatim quote and its page or location.
- Hard accuracy gate (D8): every cited quote must appear word for word in the saved source copy, allowing only whitespace and OCR punctuation differences. A separate, isolated model check of whether each sentence follows from its quote can block a sentence but never approves one on its own.
- The description carries a bibliography. Every on-screen quotation shows its source and date.

## Visual integrity

The visual style guide is a separate document. These rules are editorial and apply regardless of style (D12):

- Public-domain archival images, maps, diagrams, timelines, and document callouts carry the episode.
- Stock footage only for landscapes that have not changed. Never as narrative filler.
- No AI-generated images or video at launch.
- Never present an image as something it is not. An image of a different date, place, or person is either labeled as such or not used.
- No anachronistic uniforms, flags, borders, or buildings.
- Every asset has recorded rights. Nothing renders while a used asset's rights are unresolved.

## Synthetic voice disclosure

- Narration uses ElevenLabs voices with commercial rights: a narrator and a separate reader for primary-source quotations (D1). Not Steven's voice, and no clone of any real person without that person's consent.
- The quote voice reads only verbatim quotations, so evidence sounds distinct from narration.
- Every upload sets YouTube's altered or synthetic content disclosure. The pipeline cannot produce a release without that flag.
- Each description carries one plain line, for example: Narration uses AI-generated voices. Script and research by Tales From Time.

## Release authority

- Steven approves every episode at final review. The pipeline uploads as private at most and can never set a video public (D11).
- Changes to this charter need Steven's approval and are recorded in docs/decisions.md.
